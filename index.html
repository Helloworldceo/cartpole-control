How this works

Physics: the cart-pole is the standard nonlinear rigid-rod-on-cart model (frictionless pivot). State is [x, ẋ, θ, θ̇]; the only input is a horizontal force F on the cart. It's integrated with RK4 at a fixed timestep, sub-stepped independently of the animation frame rate.

PID: drives the pole angle to 0 with F = Kp·θ + Ki·∫θ + Kd·θ̇, plus a slow position-recovery term + Kx·x + Kdx·ẋ to stop the cart drifting off the track. Angle-only PID balances beautifully but leaves cart position uncontrolled — that coupling is exactly what makes this system harder than two independent single-loop problems, and exactly what LQR handles properly.

LQR: the nonlinear dynamics are linearized around the upright equilibrium numerically (central finite differences on the true nonlinear model — no hand-derived linear equations), giving matrices A, B. These are discretized at the simulation timestep and fed into the discrete-time algebraic Riccati equation, solved by iterating the backward Riccati recursion to convergence. The result is a single optimal gain vector K such that F = -K·(state − equilibrium) minimizes a cost trading off state error (weighted by Q) against control effort (weighted by R) — one unified law for all four state variables at once, recomputed live whenever Q, R, or the physical parameters change.

MPC: instead of one fixed gain forever (LQR), Model Predictive Control re-solves a short optimization problem every single step: "given where I am right now, what's the best sequence of forces over the next N steps?" — using the same linearized model, and the same Q/R cost, as LQR (in fact, using LQR's own cost-to-go matrix P as the value of ending up in some state at the end of that short horizon makes the two mathematically agree exactly when nothing is constrained). It only ever applies the *first* force from that plan, then throws the rest away and re-plans next step (this is what "receding horizon" means). The one thing MPC can do that LQR fundamentally can't: it knows about the force limit while planning, not just after — so instead of computing an ideal force and clipping it if it's too big (which can destabilize a system that's being pushed to its limits), MPC searches only over sequences that respect the limit from the start.

Swing-Up: a fourth, completely different design philosophy — nonlinear energy-shaping control. Instead of a linear correction near one equilibrium, it works far from equilibrium: starting from hanging straight down, it computes the pole's mechanical energy E = ½Iθ̇² + mgl(cosθ−1) (zero exactly at the upright target) and applies F = −k·θ̇·cosθ·(E_target − E) − Kc·x — a term that pumps energy into the swing every cycle until it's near the top, plus a small cart-centering term so the cart doesn't run off the rail while swinging. Once the angle and angular velocity are both small enough, it automatically hands off to LQR to catch and hold it. (Getting the energy-pumping sign and the centering term right took genuine trial and error against a numerical simulation, not just the textbook formula — a good reminder that even "solved" control problems need verification, not just derivation.)

Manual: the "Off" tab is also a playable mode — click the page and use the arrow keys to push the cart yourself. It's a good way to feel how fast and precise a real controller has to be; most people can't hold it up for more than a couple of seconds.

Try this: push the pole hard with PID position-centering gain at 0 — the pole recovers but the cart wanders. Then switch to LQR and push equally hard — position and angle recover together. Or run Test Step Response under PID vs. LQR with equivalent gains and compare settling time and overshoot directly. For MPC specifically: lower the Force limit to something quite tight (e.g. 3–5N), push the pole, and watch the "Planned force sequence" readout — MPC is visibly budgeting its limited force across the next N steps rather than just slamming into a ceiling.
